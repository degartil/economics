> "The history of Western civilization is the record of a ceaseless struggle for liberty."

## Chapter 1: The roots of the anticapitalistic mentality

> An impossible ambition is easier to forgive than a possible ambition left unrealized.

### The sovereign consumer

Mises begins with the sovereignty of the consumer, which he describes as something that "deproletarianizes the common man and elevates him to the rank of bourgeois." Much of the common discourse against capitalism presents it as a system created for the bourgeois at the expense of the common man.

Mises argues almost the opposite.

There certainly is a minority that becomes incredibly rich under capitalism, but this minority becomes rich by serving the masses and satisfying their desires. Big business is necessarily mass business. You do not create an industrial empire selling three pairs of shoes to an aristocrat; you do it by selling millions of them to ordinary people, even if at a much lower price.

But the consumer is also never satisfied.

It does not really matter that an ordinary person today may enjoy material conditions inaccessible even to the richest kings a few centuries ago. People do not usually measure their lives against distant historical poverty. They compare themselves with yesterday, with what they expect tomorrow and especially with the people around them.

An American who considers himself poor is unlikely to comfort himself by comparing his life with somebody in a desperately poor country. Someone in a moderately poor country does not normally compare himself with somebody living in absolute destitution either. What we already possess quickly becomes normal, while what remains beyond us stays visible.

Wealth is blinding.

### Ambition and resentment

This "impulsive lust" for better conditions is probably one of the fuels of capitalism itself.

The same dissatisfaction can produce very different responses. One person looks at his condition and thinks things can be better, so he saves, studies, creates, moves, invests or takes some risk. Another thinks things should already be better for him, and therefore somebody must be responsible for the fact that they are not.

The contrast becomes clearer in a status society. "The wealth of an aristocrat is not a market phenomenon." The aristocrat is wealthy and important independently of whether the masses like him. Even if he loses much of his fortune he remains a noble, and if a peasant becomes richer he does not slowly become a duke.

This is why comparisons between the rigid classes of the past and modern income brackets are so misleading. They are not different measures of the same thing. Income is a position while rank was an identity.

Capitalism destroyed much of this fixed social order and replaced it with something considerably less comfortable: mobility.

In a caste society, failure has an obvious explanation. "In a society based on caste and status, the individual can ascribe adverse fate to conditions beyond his control." If you were born a slave, there is a very clear reason why you did not become a duke. It was simply impossible.

A society with greater legal and economic mobility removes some of these explanations. "Everybody knows very well that there are people like himself who have succeeded where he himself failed."

The successful person who hurts the most is therefore not necessarily the billionaire living in another world. It is often the person who sat next to you at school, came from roughly the same conditions and ended up somewhere you wanted to be.

His existence makes it harder to believe that the door was completely closed.

Luck, illness, family circumstances, discrimination, economic conditions and countless other forces can shape a life. But the more room a society leaves for individual movement, the harder it becomes to completely remove one's own decisions from the explanation.

And when the result is painful, an explanation that places responsibility somewhere else can become very attractive.

### From birth to brains

The intellectual provides the clearest case. He may be intelligent, educated and genuinely accomplished, but society does not necessarily use those qualities as its measure of economic reward or social prestige.

Market value, intellectual value and aesthetic value are different things. There are scientists, engineers, writers, artists and philosophers whose work matters enormously even if most people could not explain what they did.

The problem begins when distinction according to one measure is treated as a claim to distinction according to another.

The aristocrat believes birth and refinement entitle him to a superior position. The intellectual may believe intelligence, education and cultural achievement should do the same. But the market does not measure human worth. It measures, much more narrowly, what other people are willing to give up for what somebody provides them.

As Mises puts it, a person is free to dedicate his life to poetry or philosophy, but this does not oblige everybody else to reward him as highly as someone serving millions of people.

The resentment appears when society seems to be using the wrong meter.

The white-collar worker encounters a related problem, but proximity makes the comparison strike harder. "Sitting behind a desk and committing words and figures to paper, he is prone to overrate the significance of his work." The prestige attached to office work can encourage somebody to see his position as more important than the market does.

But he also spends his working life near people who look much like himself and have gone further.

The comparison is built into the workplace itself. He may be the manager in the next office, the colleague who was promoted, or somebody with a similar education and background who now earns considerably more.

The same proximity that makes success seem attainable also makes failure harder to ignore.

The "cousins" are stranger still. Mises uses the term for members of wealthy families who benefit from a family enterprise without controlling it: brothers, uncles, nieces, in-laws and others who receive income or even own part of the business while somebody else actually runs it.

Their resentment can combine proximity with something resembling aristocratic entitlement.

He may enjoy the wealth produced by the enterprise while resenting the person who controls it. Material benefit is not enough when what he believes was denied to him is status, authority and recognition.

The cousin can become an anticapitalist while quite literally living from capital.

Capitalism may have enriched him but did not give him the place he believed was already his.

### The fear of tomorrow

With performers like those on Broadway and in Hollywood, fear becomes much more important.

"The essence of entertainment is variety."

A manufacturer producing for a relatively persistent need can at least expect people to continue wanting some version of what he sells. A performer depends much more directly on changing attention and taste.

He can be loved one year and forgotten the next. The audience may stop wanting what he offers precisely because it has already seen enough of it. Past success gives him no claim on future applause.

Consumer sovereignty is liberating for the consumer but merciless for somebody whose livelihood depends on remaining interesting to the audience.

The artist may genuinely fear the market, constantly feeling that he hangs by a thread. Even when he has money and recognition, neither feels permanent. Success buys time, not security, and he may imagine that abolishing capitalism will also abolish that insecurity. It is a mistake to reduce his hatred to mere envy. 

But eliminating consumer judgment does not eliminate judgment itself.

Somebody will still decide which books are printed, which films are produced, which theaters remain open and which artists receive resources. The difference is that instead of many audiences, publishers, studios and investors there may be a ministry or committee.

The number of doors through which one can escape rejection is simply reduced.

Mises gives envy a large role, but his own examples reveal a broader picture of frustrated ambition, lost status, entitlement and fear.

Capitalism gives people the opportunity to rise while refusing to guarantee that they will remain there.

It lets the consumer judge and forces the producer to keep earning his place.

A free system does not promise to value you as highly tomorrow as it does today.

## Chapter 2: The ordinary man's social philosophy

> Progress has no function of time.

Mises argues that even among people who call themselves liberals, very few properly understand the operation of a market economy.

Economics is an unusual subject. It is not exactly a natural science where one can simply isolate a phenomenon in a laboratory, but neither is it merely history or jurisprudence. It deals with systems of people reacting to prices, incentives, constraints and one another. Causes can travel through long chains of consequences before returning somewhere unexpected.

This makes economic questions unusually dependent on context. An isolated outcome may look simple while the process that produced it is not.

This ignorance leads ordinary people to make one especially important mistake. They experience enormous improvements in their material conditions without associating them with the economic order under which they occurred. Instead, they assume that "there prevails in the course of human history a self-acting tendency towards progressing."

Time passed everywhere at the same rate, yet development did not.

Ancient civilizations did not become prosperous simply by growing older. Colonial origins did not condemn countries to the same fate, nor did European ancestry or inherited institutions guarantee the same result. China and Japan followed radically different trajectories across long stretches of their histories, while countries in the Americas sharing important elements of European and colonial inheritance diverged enormously from one another.

Age, ancestry, historical prestige and the passage of time explain remarkably little by themselves.

Very different peoples, starting from very different conditions, have nevertheless achieved extraordinary material progress when the institutions and organization of production allowed it.

Knowledge and prosperity are different problems. A society may even be advanced and educated enough to discover a principle, yet lack the institutions and accumulated capital necessary to put it into production.

A market economy matters in coordinating the process that turns knowledge into production.

"Every step forward on the way to prosperity is the effect of saving."

Resources that are not immediately consumed can instead become tools, factories, machines, infrastructure and other forms of capital that make future labor more productive.

This is also why "the market process provides the common man the opportunity to enjoy the fruits of other people's achievements."

An ordinary person can make use of an absurd amount of technological and industrial complexity without understanding or producing almost any of it. He does not need to invent the transistor, manufacture the processor, lay an undersea cable or build the electrical grid. Through exchange he can gain access to the accumulated result of millions of other people's work.

Innovation itself usually begins as a luxury. The first versions of a new product are expensive, inefficient and restricted to a minority. This becomes easy evidence for the idea that capitalism only serves the rich.

Then capital accumulates, production expands, competitors appear, manufacturing improves and the luxury becomes normal, or even expected.

Once that happens the accomplishment stops being attributed to capitalism at all.

The expensive primitive version is blamed on capitalism for being inaccessible to the poor, while the cheap mature version becomes simply "technology progressing."

The benefits disappear into the background while the remaining scarcity stays visible.

### The anticapitalistic front

A system becomes very difficult to defend when its successes are treated as inevitable and its failures as uniquely its own.

This allows people with very different grievances to gather around the same opposition.

The aristocrat can resent the destruction of inherited status. The intellectual can resent consumer judgment. The worker can fear insecurity. The theologian may object for moral reasons. The socialist can believe exploitation is inherent to private ownership.

They do not need to agree on what capitalism does wrong, why it does it or what should replace it, as long as they agree that it should be destroyed.

## Chapter 3: Literature under capitalism

> "The common man does not appreciate uncommon books."

### The common and the uncommon

The literary market presents another version of consumer sovereignty. Capitalism allowed writers to make a living by selling to ordinary people instead of depending entirely on a noble patron, a church or some other institution willing to support them. This enormously expanded the market for literature, but obviously did not guarantee great literature.

If the common man wants cheap romance, detective stories, self-help, terrible fantasy or whatever else, publishers will happily produce mountains of it. This is not exactly an argument against capitalism. It is simply the consequence of allowing the public to choose. 

Market value is not artistic value. A novel selling a million copies does not mathematically become better than one read by five hundred people. "There is no yardstick to measure the aesthetic worth of a poem." The market tells us that people are willing to pay for something, not whether humanity should preserve it for the next five centuries.

This creates a real problem for uncommon authors. In the literary market Mises knew, publishing required capital, a production run, physical distribution, shelf space and enough readers to justify the whole operation. A brilliant book with a few hundred potential readers scattered around the world might simply not be economically worth printing.

The common man may not appreciate uncommon books, but it matters less and less as the market itself develops. A market large and developed enough can make even very small groups of uncommon readers economically visible. The same expansion of production and distribution that created enormous markets for popular literature can eventually make it profitable to serve audiences that would once have been too small or scattered to matter.

The uncommon does not become common, but much easier to serve.

### The capitalist villain

A recurring literary fantasy presents an immensely respected businessman as secretly monstrous while only the exceptional protagonist sees through him. It is a power trip: everyone else is fooled, while the protagonist (and by extension the reader) occupies the enlightened position of seeing what others cannot. For someone frustrated by his own failed ambitions, there can be some comfort in believing that, if he has not succeeded, he is at least one of the few who understands that the successful people around him never deserved their success.

Contradictions often appear in stories where businessmen become rich by selling rotten food, cracked steel, shoes with cardboard soles and similar garbage. "It never occurred to these authors that their narration implicitly describes all other Americans as perfect idiots whom every rascal can easily dupe."

The capitalist is often presented as a useless parasite who produces nothing anybody should value, while at the same time becoming enormously successful because consumers voluntarily keep buying what he sells.

Fraud, monopoly and political privilege can obviously explain some cases. But if the explanation is merely that the product is terrible and the businessman contributes nothing, then his continued success still needs explaining.

Repeated voluntary exchange does not prove moral virtue, nor can it simply be treated as irrelevant to explaining why somebody became rich.

## Interlude: The never-ending capitalist cycle of destruction

> Success destroys the conditions that made the previous success valuable.

### Economic displacement and innovation

If ten workers are required to produce one hundred units of something and a new machine allows two workers to produce the same amount, it is tempting to say that the machine has "destroyed eight jobs."

But this does not necessarily follow.

Greater productivity lowers the amount of labor required for each unit of output, but it can also lower costs and prices. Lower prices may increase demand enough for the industry to absorb much of its new productive capacity. If people now want five times as much of the product, all ten workers may remain necessary while producing vastly more than before.

Sometimes demand expands that much. Sometimes it does not.

When productivity grows faster than demand, fewer workers are required even while total production increases.

This distinction matters because technological progress does not consist simply of machines replacing people. It consists in obtaining more results from fewer scarce resources. Society does not want work for the sake of work; it wants whatever the work produces.

If employment itself were the objective, economic policy would be incredibly easy. We could ban excavators and employ hundreds of men with shovels, or even better, spoons. We could destroy washing machines and create an enormous laundry industry. We could ban spreadsheets and return armies of clerks to calculating columns by hand.

There would certainly be plenty of work, just not of wealth.

The difficulty is that an economy does not experience productivity gains as one unified person. Consumers may receive more or cheaper goods while the workers no longer required to produce them receive termination letters.

This is what makes economic displacement much more serious than simply saying that workers are afraid of change. The displaced worker may have invested years accumulating something very similar to physical capital: specialized knowledge, experience, reputation and skill.

Then new capital makes much of the same output cheaper to produce.

The worker did not become less intelligent or forget his skill. The work may still be useful. People may still want the output.

They may simply no longer value having a human being produce it enough to pay the old price.

What changed was its scarcity.

Looking backward from a rich industrial society, the image of the luddites opposing mechanized textile production seems obviously absurd. From the perspective of a worker watching his livelihood collapse, the machine looked considerably less benevolent.

The Luddites could be wrong about the machine while being completely right about what the machine was doing to them.

For most of the industrial era automation was something educated professionals could watch happen to somebody else. Machines replaced physical labor, clerical work was computerized and entire occupations changed. Software engineers could even be the people writing the automation.

Then the machine started writing software.

If an LLM can produce increasingly large portions of implementation cheaply, whether this reduces the total number of programmers depends on whether demand expands enough to absorb the increase in productivity.

Not every programmer displaced from routine work becomes an architect earning twice as much money, nor does every illustrator become an art director supervising machines. Real displacement can mean unemployment, retraining, lower wages, moving to another industry or discovering that years of accumulated human capital no longer command the return they once did.

Fear of automation can be completely rational while opposition to automation can still be mistaken.

An economic system that preserved the value of every profession forever would have to prevent most technological progress.

Workers, entrepreneurs and investors try different uses for the newly available resources. Some succeed and many fail. New industries bid for labor and capital while old ones contract.

Nobody can know the complete destination beforehand.

Joseph Schumpeter gave this process its famous name: Creative Destruction.

A successful innovation changes what is worth producing, what skills are valuable, what kind of capital is useful and even what can economically exist at all.

The economy discovers where scarcity has moved.

The gain and the pain are both real, but for some people the pain is immediate and overwhelming.

Capitalism can therefore create some of its own enemies.

### The reorganization of production

Creative destruction does not stop at the labor market.

A new technology can reduce the value of machinery, factories, raw materials, infrastructure and entire supply chains built around the previous way of producing something. An innovation may make fewer workers necessary, but it can also make a particular machine obsolete, reduce demand for a fuel or commodity, eliminate the need for specialized suppliers or turn enormously valuable capital into junk.

At every step of the way, there can be unemployment.

But this destruction is only one side of the process. The same changes that make old productive arrangements less valuable can make entirely new ones economical. Something once scarce and expensive can become cheap enough to disappear into the infrastructure underneath another activity.

The literary problem Mises describes provides a perfect example. What once required a print run, physical distribution and shelf space can now ride on infrastructure already operating at enormous scale.

Datacenters, fiber-optic networks, computers, payment systems and digital storefronts operate at enormous scale underneath the individual book, while the marginal cost of distributing another digital copy is approximately nothing.

"The common man does not appreciate uncommon books" may remain perfectly true, but it matters considerably less when uncommon people can cheaply find one another.

The market became so large and its infrastructure so cheap that tiny markets could survive inside it, piggybacking on infrastructure developed for much larger uses.

This did not merely make existing books cheaper. It changed which books could economically exist in the first place. A work no publisher would have considered worth printing can now be distributed globally at almost no cost.

The same pattern may now be appearing in intellectual production. For now, much of the machinery underneath it remains highly centralized. An AI system may provide an intensely individualized output while the computation producing it occurs in enormous datacenters containing expensive accelerators and consuming substantial amounts of electricity.

The output has begun moving toward the individual before the machinery has.

There is no reason to assume this is the final arrangement. If sufficiently capable hardware and models continue becoming cheaper, more of this productive capacity may itself move toward smaller organizations or individual users, much as computation moved from institutional machines toward personal computers. And when that happens, the centralized AI systems that dissolved the value of intellectual work may see their own value dissolved by much the same process.

This is no neat exchange.

Creative destruction reorganizes production but does not compensate every participant in the previous arrangement. 

Success destroys the conditions that made the previous success valuable.

Then the process begins again.

## Chapter 4: The non-economic objections to capitalism

> "If this is unjust then blame rests with nature and not with man."

### The argument of happiness

One of the stranger accusations against capitalism is that material goods do not produce happiness.

Mises writes that "people do not toil and trouble in order to attain perfect happiness but in order to remove as much as possible some felt uneasiness and thus become happier than before."

Capitalism cannot promise happiness because happiness is not something a factory can produce. A washing machine will not give somebody meaning in life. Neither will a car, an apartment or antibiotics.

But being homeless in the rain does not exactly improve your odds of happiness either.

Material wealth can remove particular sources of misery. A parent whose child survives because modern medicine reduced child mortality has not been granted perfect happiness, but an enormous possible source of suffering has been removed.

### Novelty versus nostalgia

The accusation of materialism is similarly strange. Critics "cannot help admitting that capitalism has the tendency to improve the material conditions of mankind. But, they say, it has diverted men from the higher and nobler pursuits."

If anything, not spending most of the day trying to avoid starvation leaves considerably more time for "nobler" pursuits.

But who decides what those pursuits are?

"There is no yardstick to measure the aesthetic worth of a poem."

The reactionary looks toward masters of the past and sees modern art as decline, while often showing little sympathy for contemporary artists actually attempting something new. But history performs its own selection. We remember the masters much more than the mountains of mediocre garbage that existed around them.

The past reaches us filtered through what survived.

The present does not enjoy that luxury.

Much of the supposed conflict between capitalism and culture is therefore better understood as a conflict between novelty and nostalgia.

His architectural example makes the same point. In his environment, government buildings often imitated established historical styles while private apartments, offices and commercial buildings were more willing to experiment with contemporary ones.

Novelty has a peculiar advantage under capitalism because doing something nobody has done before can itself become an opportunity.

### Misfortune is not injustice

Mises writes that "the worst of all these delusions is the idea that 'nature' has bestowed upon every man certain rights." Nature contains no mechanism distributing wealth according to some moral rule. It does not assign houses, food, income or opportunities according to what each person deserves.

If another person has something I do not, that fact alone does not establish that somebody wronged me.

There can be real injustices produced by theft, coercion, discrimination or institutions. But inequality is not by itself proof of injustice, just as an unfortunate outcome is not proof that somebody caused it wrongfully.

"If this is unjust then blame rests with nature and not with man."

### Capital accumulation and wages

The iron law of wages holds, in its harsher form, that improvements in production cannot permanently raise workers above subsistence because additional income will produce population growth until wages are pushed back down again.

Mises's objection is straightforward: humans are not rats. Greater wealth does not automatically turn into enough additional children to consume every improvement in living standards. People can spend additional income on larger houses, education, entertainment, savings and countless other things.

"What raises wage rates and allots to the wage earners an ever increasing portion out of the output which has been enhanced by additional capital accumulation is the fact that the rate of capital accumulation exceeds the rate of increase in population."

The effect of this capital accumulation extends far beyond the workers who actually use the new capital.

"Increase in wage rates does not depend on the individual worker's 'productivity' but on the marginal productivity of labor."

Suppose factories equipped with increasing amounts of capital can profitably employ another worker at a relatively high wage because machinery makes his labor more productive. Somebody who wants to employ that same person as a private butler must now compete with the factory for his time.

Nothing has made the butler himself better at serving dinner.

What changed is his alternative.

If he can earn more elsewhere, keeping him as a butler requires paying enough to make giving up that opportunity worthwhile. Capital accumulation in an entirely different industry has therefore raised the price of his labor without the butler ever touching the machinery responsible for it.

The same applies to cleaners, waiters, barbers and countless other workers whose own jobs may use very little capital. Their wages are constrained not only by what they produce in their current job, but by what somebody else would be willing to pay for the marginal use of their labor.

### Forever rebel

For Mises, Western civilization is defined by a "ceaseless struggle for liberty."

From the Greeks onward, the history he describes is filled with attempts to place limits on authority and carve out a sphere in which the individual cannot simply be commanded. The West has not always been free. It has had kings, slaves, serfs, inquisitions, dictators and every other form of coercion. But the ideal of the free man survived all of them.

Again and again the hero of the Western story is the person who refuses to accept that authority is legitimate merely because it already exists: the subject who challenges the king, the dissenter who refuses the established doctrine, the citizen who insists that even government must justify its power.

Freedom became part of how the West understood itself.

Even the meaning of "the West" eventually became less geographical than ideological. Japan sits about as far east as one can go, yet it is often grouped politically and economically with the West because it is a wealthy liberal democracy integrated into the same broad order. Geography alone no longer explains the category very well.

Race does not explain it either. Religion, ancestry and history certainly helped shape the civilization from which these ideas emerged, but the Western ideal of liberty became something that could be adopted rather than merely inherited.

Capitalism institutionalizes rebellion in the economic order. No wonder it was born in the West.

The existing producer has no permanent right to remain the producer. The traditional way of doing something receives no protection simply because it is traditional. A nobody can decide that an entire industry is doing something badly, build an alternative and attempt to replace it without first being appointed by anybody.

Ever heard of anything more rebellious than innovation?

Mises denounces what he calls "super-liberalism." Socialism, planning and the welfare state can present themselves not as restrictions on liberty but as attempts to complete it. Formal freedom is supposedly insufficient; freedom must include claims to particular material outcomes.

They mistake liberty for power.

A right to speak primarily requires that others be prevented from silencing you. A right to receive a particular material good raises another question: somebody must produce, finance or surrender that good.

The danger is in calling both things liberty, because the second can require coercion in the name of expanding the first.

But even the older liberal conception of liberty needs something underneath it.

"The constitution and bills of rights do not create freedom. They merely protect freedom that the competitive system grants to individuals against encroachments on the part of the police power."

A constitution is merely words on paper.

Declaring freedom of the press means little if the state owns every printing press, every building where one could operate one, every source of financing and every means of distribution. The law can say that I am free to publish while the structure underneath gives me nowhere independent from which to do it.

That is why decentralization matters for liberty even beyond its economic usefulness. A centralized system creates a single point whose refusal can become final. A competitive system cannot guarantee that anybody will believe me, employ me or support what I want to do, but it makes it much harder for one authority to make that decision for everyone else.

The rebel needs somewhere else to go.

## Chapter 5: Anticommunism versus capitalism

The intellectual who dreams of replacing vulgar consumer judgment with enlightened political planning quietly assumes that he will be one of the enlightened planners.

There is no reason for this to be true: "enthusiastic endorsement of the totalitarian ideology did not guarantee immunity from torture and execution."

Once independent publishers, employers, institutions and sources of income disappear, the loyal intellectual has also destroyed many of the places where he could have escaped if political authority decided tomorrow that his ideas were no longer acceptable.

Mises argues that anticommunism cannot itself become a positive program. Agreement about what should be destroyed says nothing about what should replace it.

Every utopian movement attempts in some way to "put an end to history." Once the correct arrangement is established, the fundamental social contradictions will supposedly disappear and humanity can finally settle into the solved system.

But history has no final state and human development has no predetermined direction.

A liberal society leaves room for history to continue.

> "Freedom is indivisible."
